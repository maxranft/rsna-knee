# Learning-curve diagnostics ("delta" / "gamma"): established ML methods, pitfalls, and epoch-budget recommendations

Scope: the project's dashboard defines **delta** = validation macro AUC gained per epoch (first difference, 3-epoch moving mean, averaged over 5 folds), **gamma** = change in delta per epoch (second difference), and calls a run **"flat"** once delta < 0.001 AUC/epoch. Training is a small attention head on cached frozen features, 12–28 epochs per fold, AdamW + PyTorch `OneCycleLR` (10% warmup, cosine anneal to ~0). Sources were checked 2026-10-16; dates are given for each.

## 1. Early-stopping criteria in the literature, and how a "first difference below threshold" rule compares

### Takeaway
The classic criteria (Prechelt 1997/1998: GL, PQ, UP; library "patience + min_delta" rules) all compare the current validation score with the **best-so-far** score and return the **best-so-far weights**. A rule that fires when the smoothed per-epoch slope drops below a threshold is closest to Prechelt's **training-progress** measure P_k. Prechelt used that measure only as a termination fallback, applied to *training* error, and never as the selection rule. Prechelt's own results show the choice of criterion trades training time for small gains in generalization (about 4% lower error for about 4x the training time).

### Cited Findings
- Prechelt defines **generalization loss** GL(t) = 100·(E_va(t)/E_opt(t) − 1), the percentage rise of validation error over the minimum so far. Criterion **GL_α** says "stop after first epoch t with GL(t) > α". — [Prechelt, "Early Stopping — but when?" (preprint 1997; in *Neural Networks: Tricks of the Trade*, LNCS 1524, 1998)](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- **Training progress** over a strip of k epochs: P_k(t) = 1000·(Σ_{t'=t−k+1..t} E_tr(t') / (k·min_{t'} E_tr(t')) − 1), "how much was the average training error during the strip larger than the minimum training error during the strip?". It is "guaranteed to approach zero in the long run unless the training is globally unstable". **PQ_α**: stop after the first end-of-strip epoch with GL(t)/P_k(t) > α. Strips have length k = 5, and validation error is measured only at the end of each strip. — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- **UP_s**: stop when validation error increased in s successive strips. It relies "only on the sign of the changes", "independent of how large the increases actually are". — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- Because no criterion guarantees termination, Prechelt adds the rule that "training is stopped when the progress drops below 0.1 or after at most 3000 epochs". This is the closest analogue to a "flat" rule, but it measures training error and acts only as a fallback. Every criterion returns "the set of weights that exhibited the lowest validation error E_opt(t)". — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- "Real validation error curves almost always have more than one local minimum." The example curve has 16 local minima before overfitting at about epoch 400. Stopping at epoch 400 instead of shortly after the first deep minimum (epoch 45) "trades an about sevenfold increase of learning time for an improvement of validation set error by 1.1%". — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- Experiment: 12 topologies, 12 tasks (PROBEN1), 14 criteria, 1,296 runs. Result: "slower" criteria lead to better generalization on average, but "the training time … is rather large on average and also varies dramatically", and "the systematic differences between the criteria classes are only small". The rules of thumb are: use fast criteria unless ~4% gains are worth ~4x training time; use GL to maximise the probability of a good solution; to maximise average quality, use PQ if the net overfits only very little, otherwise UP. — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- Quantified: "we can roughly trade a 4% decrease in test error (from 1.024 to 0.988) for an about fourfold increase in training time (from 0.766 to 3.095)". Even the fastest criteria "waste" about one fifth of their training time, and slower criteria train twice as long as needed. Fast criteria reach a result as good as the best of the same run in about 60% of cases, and the slowest in about 80%. Best time/quality trade-offs for a single run are UP_3, UP_4 and UP_6. — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- For networks with very few hidden nodes, "the PQ criteria are more cost-effective" because "such small networks do not overfit severely; in this case it is advantageous to take training progress into account". — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- The Keras `EarlyStopping` callback (current docs): `min_delta` is "Minimum change in the monitored quantity to qualify as an improvement, i.e. an absolute change of less than min_delta, will count as no improvement"; `patience` is the "number of epochs with no improvement after which training will be stopped"; `restore_best_weights` restores "the epoch with the best value"; `start_from_epoch` gives "a warm-up period in which no improvement is expected". — [Keras docs](https://keras.io/api/callbacks/early_stopping/)

### Inferences
- The project's "flat" rule (smoothed slope < 0.001/epoch) differs from the standard rules in three ways. (1) It compares with the **previous few epochs**, not the **best so far**, so it can fire during a noisy dip or a temporary plateau. (2) It has **no patience**, apart from the implicit 3-epoch smoothing. (3) It returns no best-weights checkpoint. In standard terms it is a **plateau / convergence detector**, like Prechelt's progress fallback or a `min_delta` threshold, rather than an overfitting detector like GL or UP.
- Prechelt's measures are relative: GL in percent, P_k in per-mille. A fixed absolute threshold of 0.001 AUC/epoch means a different thing at AUC 0.70 than at 0.92, because the remaining headroom differs. A relative form, e.g. delta / (1 − AUC), would be the analogue of Prechelt's scale-free definitions.
- Prechelt worked with constant-schedule backprop in 1997, before LR annealing schedules were common, so his numbers (4% for 4x time) are a qualitative guide only (see section 3).

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that compares "slope-below-threshold" stopping directly with patience-based stopping for deep nets under annealed schedules.
- I did not re-fetch the PyTorch Lightning / `ReduceLROnPlateau` docs. Their `threshold` / `patience` semantics follow the same best-so-far pattern, but I did not verify them in this session.

## 2. Learning-curve extrapolation and modelling: can final performance be predicted from early epochs?

### Takeaway
There is a mature literature on predicting the end of an epoch-wise learning curve from its beginning. It includes parametric ensembles with MCMC (Domhan et al. 2015), freeze-thaw GP kernels (Swersky et al. 2014), PFN transformers (LC-PFN, 2023), and LR-schedule-aware loss laws (Tissue et al. 2024). These methods give ~2–6x HPO speedups by **terminating clearly bad runs**. They are not used to decide when a single good run is "done". They also assume monotone, saturating curves, an assumption the annealing phase of a OneCycle/cosine schedule distorts. The survey literature stresses that no single functional form fits all curves.

### Cited Findings
- Domhan, Springenberg & Hutter (IJCAI 2015) model validation performance as y_t = Σ_k w_k f_k(t|θ_k) + ε. They use **11 parametric curve families** from the literature, including vapor pressure, pow3, Hill3, log power, MMF, exp4, Janoschek, Weibull and ilog2, and fit them with MCMC (emcee). — [Domhan et al. 2015](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/487.pdf)
- Their prior "explicitly encode[s] … that well-behaved learning curves are increasing saturating functions". Weights are positive, so "the predicted curve will only be flat once all models have flattened out". — [Domhan et al. 2015](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/487.pdf)
- Their **predictive termination criterion** stops a run when P(y_m ≥ best-so-far | y_1:n) < δ, with δ = 0.05 ("95% certain that it would not improve over the best known performance"). It is checked every p = 30 epochs, with max epochs e.g. 285. The result: it "consistently sped up two state-of-the-art hyperparameter optimizers by a factor of roughly two". — [Domhan et al. 2015](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/487.pdf)
- Freeze-Thaw Bayesian Optimization (Swersky, Snoek & Adams, arXiv 2014) uses "a novel positive-definite covariance kernel to capture a variety of training curves" to decide whether to pause or resume partially trained models. — [Swersky et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.3896) (the abstract page does not state the kernel's exact form, an exponential-decay basis, so that detail is unverified here)
- LC-PFN (Adriaensen, Rakotoarison, Müller & Hutter, arXiv Oct 2023) is a transformer trained on "10 million artificial right-censored learning curves" from a parametric prior. It is "over 10 000 times faster" than MCMC, approximates the posterior predictive "more accurately than MCMC", and was evaluated on 20,000 real curves (LCBench, NAS-Bench-201, Taskset, PD1; 53 datasets). A simple LC-PFN predictive early-stopping criterion "obtains 2 - 6x speed-ups on 45 of these datasets". — [Adriaensen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20447)
- Viering & Loog's review "The Shape of Learning Curves" (arXiv 2021, rev. Nov 2022) treats learning curves as performance vs **training-set size** (sample-wise, not epoch-wise). It finds "well-behaved curves that often have the shape of a power law or an exponential", that curves are "surprisingly diverse", and that "no universal model can be identified". The revision adds "more scepticism towards the idea that it has been proven" that power laws fit. — [Viering & Loog](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.10948) (the arXiv page does not list the journal; the brief cites IEEE TPAMI, which I did not verify)
- Tissue, Wang & Wang (arXiv Aug 2024, LLM pre-training) fit **L(s) = L0 + A·S1^(−α) − C·S2**. S1 is the "forward area" under the LR curve and S2 the "annealing area". By "fitting only one or two training curves, we can accurately predict the loss at any given step" "across any learning rate scheduler". — [Tissue et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11029)

### Inferences
- In this literature, the **first-difference "delta" is a crude, non-probabilistic version of extrapolation**: it implicitly assumes the next epoch gains about as much as the last 3. The established methods replace that with a fitted saturating curve and a probability of beating a target, which is better posed for the question "is more training worth it?".
- For 12–28 epoch curves under OneCycle, Domhan-style "increasing, saturating" priors are mis-specified. The curve's shape depends on the LR schedule, so curvature changes during warmup and gains bunch up in the anneal. Tissue et al.'s formulation is the only one found that conditions on the schedule: progress is driven by S1 (cumulative LR) plus a bonus proportional to annealing (S2), not by epoch count. This supports measuring progress **per unit of cumulative LR**, not per epoch (see section 3).
- With minutes-long runs on cached features (S3), an extrapolation model is unnecessary for choosing an epoch budget. Running the full schedule for several budgets is cheaper and exact. Extrapolation becomes relevant only if training is moved to expensive end-to-end runs.

### Gaps
- I did not find evidence on how well Domhan or LC-PFN extrapolators work on **AUC** curves of **small heads** with **OneCycle** schedules. Their benchmarks (LCBench, etc.) mostly use fixed or simple schedules, and I did not verify the schedules used.
- The Tissue et al. law is shown for LLM cross-entropy loss. I found no validation for AUC or for small classifiers.

## 3. Interaction with learning-rate schedules: reading curves under cosine / OneCycle annealing, and comparing "epoch at which flat" across runs

### Takeaway
Under a schedule that anneals to zero at a pre-set total length, the learning curve depends on the schedule. Gains bunch up during the decay, and the per-epoch improvement is **forced toward zero at the end of every run by construction**, because the step size goes to zero. Recent work (Hägele et al., NeurIPS 2024) states that cosine-run intermediate points "underestimate" what a run of that length would achieve, and that "the training duration must be known in advance and match the cosine length". "Epoch at which flat" is therefore largely a function of the total-epoch setting. It is **not comparable across runs with different total epochs**. The meaningful comparison is the **final** (end-of-schedule) score of each budget.

### Cited Findings
- PyTorch `OneCycleLR` "changes the learning rate after every batch". It ramps up to `max_lr` and then anneals to `min_lr = initial_lr/final_div_factor`, with `initial_lr = max_lr/div_factor`. Defaults: `pct_start` 0.3, `anneal_strategy='cos'`, `div_factor` 25, `final_div_factor` 1e4. The policy comes from the Super-Convergence paper. — [PyTorch OneCycleLR docs (v2.14)](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.OneCycleLR.html)
- Smith & Topin (arXiv 2017, rev. 2018), "Super-Convergence": "One of the key elements of super-convergence is training with one learning rate cycle and a large maximum learning rate". "large learning rates regularize the training, hence requiring a reduction of all other forms of regularization". Nets "can be trained an order of magnitude faster". — [Smith & Topin](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.07120)
- Loshchilov & Hutter, SGDR (ICLR 2017): warm restarts with cosine annealing to "improve its anytime performance". — [Loshchilov & Hutter](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03983)
- Hägele et al. (NeurIPS 2024 spotlight): "cosine is suboptimal during training and underestimates the model's performance for the same token count". With cosine, "the training duration must be known in advance and match the cosine length". The authors argue research "has been needlessly complex due to reliance on the cosine schedule". — [Hägele et al. 2024, HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18392v3); [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.18392)
- Hägele et al., during cooldown: "the sudden drop in loss is consistent for both train and validation loss and aligns closely with the decay in LR". Constant LR "with a short cooldown (< 20% of total training steps) can achieve the same final loss as cosine". Gains "plateau at around 20%" cooldown, and a (1−sqrt) cooldown "outperforms the linear cooldown". — [Hägele et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18392v3)
- Defazio et al., "The Road Less Scheduled" (arXiv May 2024, rev. Oct 2024): existing schedules "that do not require specification of the optimization stopping step T are greatly out-performed" by T-dependent ones. Schedule-Free "unifies scheduling and iterate averaging", adds "no additional hyper-parameters over standard optimizers with momentum", and Schedule-Free AdamW won the MLCommons 2024 AlgoPerf self-tuning track. — [Defazio et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15682)
- Tissue et al. 2024 model loss as a function of cumulative LR area (S1) plus an annealing-area term (S2), not of step count. — [Tissue et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11029)

### Inferences
- **Delta is forced to zero by the schedule (worked numbers).** With a cosine anneal, LR at fraction f of the anneal phase is max_lr·(1+cos πf)/2 (plus a negligible floor). At f = 0.8 that is ~9.5% of peak, and at f = 0.9 ~2.4%. With 10% warmup, the last ~10% of epochs run at <2.5% of peak LR: about epoch 11–12 of a 12-epoch run, or about epochs 25.5–28 of a 28-epoch run. Parameter updates there are tiny, so delta → 0 whether or not the model has converged. A "flat" flag in the tail of a OneCycle run is a **property of the schedule, not evidence of convergence**.
- **"Epoch at which flat" scales with the total-epoch setting.** OneCycle sets LR by the fraction of total steps, so epoch e sits at a different LR phase in a 12-epoch run than in a 28-epoch run. Comparing "flat at epoch 9" (12-epoch run) with "flat at epoch 20" (28-epoch run) mostly compares schedules. A fairer x-axis is the fraction of schedule completed or cumulative LR (Tissue's S1). A fairer outcome is the end-of-schedule AUC.
- **Annealing gains can make delta rise late.** During the steepest part of the cosine decay, validation AUC can jump (Hägele's "sudden drop in loss … aligns closely with the decay in LR"). Delta and gamma will then show late positive spikes driven by the LR decay, which naive reading would take as "still learning a lot".
- **Prefer a schedule that separates "how far" from "how long" if anytime diagnostics are wanted.** Warmup → constant → short (1−sqrt) cooldown of ~20% (WSD) keeps the curve before cooldown readable. Delta is meaningful on the constant phase, and cooldowns can be forked from checkpoints of a single long run to get final-score-vs-budget for several budgets cheaply. Schedule-Free AdamW is an alternative that needs no T. Both serve **S3** (iteration speed).

### Gaps
- Almost all quantitative evidence on cooldown and annealing gains is from LLM pre-training (Hägele, Tissue). I found no study measuring the "annealing share" of final AUC for small classifier heads on frozen features trained for tens of epochs.
- I did not fetch the original WSD paper (Hu et al., MiniCPM, 2024) or "river-valley" analyses of WSD. Hägele et al. is the main citation here.

## 4. Noise in per-epoch validation AUC: smoothing, and whether first and second differences are meaningful

### Takeaway
Per-epoch validation AUC is noisy in two ways: **sampling noise** (which studies are in the fold, with small positive counts) and **optimization noise** (SGD jitter, which Prechelt explicitly notes). Consecutive epochs are scored on the same validation studies, so most sampling noise cancels in a **first difference**, though it still determines whether the level is meaningful. With 10–13 gold studies per fold, per-fold gold AUC per epoch is mostly noise. A 3-epoch trailing mean of first differences reduces exactly to a 3-epoch secant slope. A second difference of a noisy series amplifies noise, and I found **no literature using the second derivative (curvature) of a validation learning curve as a stopping diagnostic**.

### Cited Findings
- Prechelt: validation curves have many local minima (16 in his example). P_k is "high for unstable phases of training" because algorithms "sometimes produce such 'jitter' by taking inappropriately large steps in weight space". Fast criteria find a solution as good as the best of the run only ~60% of the time, showing how often a local dip or plateau misleads. — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- Hanley & McNeil (Radiology, 1982) give the standard error of an empirical AUC: SE(A) = sqrt{[A(1−A) + (n₊−1)(Q₁−A²) + (n₋−1)(Q₂−A²)]/(n₊n₋)}, with Q₁ = A/(2−A) and Q₂ = 2A²/(1+A). DeLong et al. (1988) is the common alternative (Stata's default). A 1994 simulation found Hanley–McNeil can underestimate the required sample size. — [Hanley & McNeil 1982 (PDF)](https://math.ucdavis.edu/~saito/data/roc/hanley-mcneil-auc.pdf); [Stata note sjart_st0020](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/116008/files/sjart_st0020.pdf); [innolitics summary](https://innolitics.com/articles/how-many-cases-to-power-an-auc-analysis/)
- Domhan et al. model per-step validation performance as a parametric curve **plus Gaussian noise ε ~ N(0, σ²)**. That is, the established view is that per-epoch scores are noisy observations of an underlying curve, to be modelled rather than differenced. — [Domhan et al. 2015](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/487.pdf)
- Project facts (from `docs/data.md`, aggregates only): 5 folds of ~881 studies; gold studies are 10–13 per fold; gold positives over 58 studies include MCL 9 and Lateral OA 11. The doc already notes "a CV difference below about 0.02 macro AUC is noise".

### Inferences
- **Illustrative Hanley–McNeil SEs** (computed here, single label, at AUC 0.75 / 0.85 / 0.90):
  - Per-fold report-labelled set, ~880 studies: 5% prevalence gives SE ≈ 0.043 / 0.037 / 0.031; 20% prevalence gives ≈ 0.023 / 0.019 / 0.016.
  - All 4,349 OOF report-only studies, 20% prevalence: ≈ 0.010 / 0.009 / 0.007.
  - Gold n = 58 with 15 positives: ≈ 0.080 / 0.066 / 0.056. With 5 positives: ≈ 0.13 / 0.11 / 0.094.
  - Per-fold gold (10–13 studies, 1–3 positives for some labels): one swapped positive–negative pair moves that label's AUC by roughly 0.03–0.1.

  A 0.001 AUC/epoch threshold is far below the **level** uncertainty of any of these. Macro-averaging over 12 correlated labels and 5 folds shrinks this, but by less than sqrt(60).
- **Differences are paired, which helps.** AUC at epochs t and t−1 is computed on the same studies, so the sampling component is highly correlated and largely cancels. What remains in delta is mostly optimization noise plus genuine change. Its size is an empirical question: measure it by re-running one configuration with 3–5 seeds and taking the across-seed SD of per-epoch delta. Set the "flat" threshold at ≥2x that SD, or use a paired bootstrap over validation studies of AUC(t) − AUC(t−k).
- **The 3-epoch moving mean of first differences telescopes.** mean(Δ_t, Δ_{t−1}, Δ_{t−2}) = (AUC_t − AUC_{t−3})/3. It is a secant slope over 3 epochs that ignores the two middle points and lags by ~1.5 epochs. An EMA or a local linear fit over a window uses all points and is less noisy for the same lag. Fold-averaging before differencing is the most effective smoothing available here.
- **Gamma (second difference) is not recommended as a decision signal.** Differencing white noise of variance σ² gives variance 2σ² for first differences and 6σ² for second differences. Gamma of the smoothed series is (AUC_t − AUC_{t−1} − AUC_{t−3} + AUC_{t−4})/3. On 12–28 points it is dominated by noise and by the schedule's built-in curvature (warmup bend, anneal bend). If curvature is wanted, read it off a fitted smooth curve (e.g. Domhan-style parametric or a spline vs fraction-of-schedule), not raw differences.

### Gaps
- I found no published measurement of epoch-to-epoch AUC noise for small heads on frozen features. The values above are sampling-theory bounds only; optimization noise must be measured on this project's runs.
- I found no ML paper that uses the second derivative of a validation curve as a stopping or diagnostic criterion. The brief's "curvature" item is unsupported by literature I could find.

## 5. Alternatives to picking an epoch (SWA / EMA / checkpoint averaging) and the epoch-wise double-descent pitfall; selection bias

### Takeaway
Averaging weights over the late trajectory (SWA, EMA) is the established alternative to choosing a single epoch. It is cheap, generally improves or matches the final checkpoint, and under constant LR partly replaces annealing, though it does not fully match an explicit cooldown (Hägele 2024). Two pitfalls matter here. **Epoch-wise double descent** (the validation score dips then recovers) appears mainly with label noise, which is relevant because training labels are report-derived. And **choosing the best epoch on the same validation fold that produces the OOF score** inflates CV (Cawley & Talbot 2010), which is most severe on 58 gold studies.

### Cited Findings
- SWA (Izmailov et al., UAI 2018): "simple averaging of multiple points along the trajectory of SGD, with a cyclical or constant learning rate, leads to better generalization than conventional training". It "finds much flatter solutions than SGD" and "has almost no computational overhead". — [Izmailov et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.05407)
- Hägele et al. 2024: SWA "yields improved performance along the training trajectory, without additional training costs". With constant LR, it "does not reach the loss values of explicit cooldowns", but it "reduces the gap without the need for a separate decay phase". EMA performed worse than SWA in their setting, and SWA gives "reliable (strong, but not optimal) estimates of models during runs". — [Hägele et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18392v3)
- Morales-Brotons, Vogels & Hendrikx (TMLR 2024), EMA of weights: averaging damps SGD noise, so EMA "requires less learning rate decay". It performs well early in training, and "combined with early stopping" it can skip the low-LR final phase. EMA models show better robustness to noisy labels, prediction consistency, calibration and transfer. — [Morales-Brotons et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18704)
- Nakkiran et al. (arXiv Dec 2019), "Deep Double Descent": "double descent occurs not just as a function of model size, but also as a function of the number of training epochs". The paper unifies these with "effective model complexity". — [Nakkiran et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02292)
- Stephenson & Lee (arXiv Aug 2021): "epochwise double descent requires a critical amount of noise to occur", and "above a second critical noise level early stopping remains effective". The proposed mechanism is features that are slow to learn but informative. — [Stephenson & Lee 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.12006)
- Heckel & Yilmaz ("Early Stopping in Deep Networks: Double Descent and How to Eliminate It", ICLR 2021, per search summary) attribute epoch-wise double descent to superimposed bias–variance trade-offs from parts of the network learning at different speeds. Per-layer step-size adjustment removes it and "improve[s] early stopping performance". — [search result pointing to ar5iv/arXiv listings](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2108.12006) (I did not open the Heckel & Yilmaz paper directly, so treat this as a secondary summary)
- Cawley & Talbot (JMLR 11, 2010): variance in a model-selection criterion causes over-fitting in model selection. The resulting bias "can be about the size of performance differences between learning algorithms" and applies to "any model selection practice involving the optimisation of a model selection criterion evaluated over a finite sample". — [Cawley & Talbot 2010](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html)

### Inferences
- **Selection bias in best-epoch picking.** If each fold restores its best-validation epoch and those same predictions form the pooled OOF score, the score is optimistically biased. With ~11 gold studies per fold and 0.02 already at noise level, the bias could be of the order of the differences being compared. Safer: pick **one fixed epoch count (or the end-of-schedule checkpoint) for all folds** from the pooled curve, or nest the choice.
- Under OneCycle-to-zero, the final checkpoint is typically at or near the best. A useful diagnostic is "best epoch AUC minus last epoch AUC" on the 4,349 report-labelled OOF. If it is consistently below noise, drop best-epoch restoration and use the last epoch.
- SWA or EMA over the last ~20% of epochs is cheap for a small head and directly serves **S4** (one robust small model, no extra inference cost) and **S3**. Under the current OneCycle schedule, the last-20% weights barely move, so the expected gain is small. SWA/EMA matters more if the project moves to a constant/WSD schedule.
- The report-derived (soft) labels are noisy by design (S1). Epoch-wise double descent is therefore a plausible pitfall: a mid-run dip in report-label AUC followed by recovery would trip a "flat" rule or a GL rule early. A small head on frozen features has limited capacity to memorise noise, which lowers the risk. It can be checked once with a long constant-LR run.

### Gaps
- I did not find studies of epoch-wise double descent for small attention heads on frozen features, or with soft (probabilistic) labels.
- The Heckel & Yilmaz details come from a search summary, not the paper itself.

## 6. Terminology: is "delta/gamma" (options Greeks) used in ML? What are the standard names?

### Takeaway
No ML literature found uses "delta/gamma" for learning-curve slope and curvature. The finance analogy is also mis-mapped: option **delta** and **gamma** are derivatives with respect to the **underlying price**. The derivative with respect to **time** is **theta**, and the time derivative of delta is **charm**. In ML, "delta" and "gamma" already name other things, notably Keras `min_delta`, an improvement threshold that is exactly what the "flat" rule thresholds. Standard terms are the **slope (rate of improvement) of the learning or training curve**, **progress** (Prechelt), **generalization loss**, **plateau**, and **curvature** of a fitted curve.

### Cited Findings
- Options: delta is "the first partial derivative of value with respect to the underlying", Δ = ∂V/∂S, and gamma is "the partial derivative of delta with respect to the underlying price", Γ = ∂²V/∂S². — [Brilliant: Option Greeks – Gamma](https://brilliant.org/wiki/option-greeks-gamma/); [MathWorks: Plotting sensitivities of an option](https://au.mathworks.com/help/finance/plotting-sensitivities-of-an-option.html)
- Theta "is the first derivative of option value with respect to time[-to-expiration]". **Charm** ("delta decay") is ∂Δ/∂t = ∂θ/∂S, quoted as change in delta per day. (These are retail and trading-education sources; the core definitions agree across them.) — [Macroption: Option charm](https://www.macroption.com/option-charm); [daytrading.com: Theta](https://daytrading.com/theta-option-greek)
- Keras uses `min_delta` for the "minimum change in the monitored quantity to qualify as an improvement". — [Keras EarlyStopping](https://keras.io/api/callbacks/early_stopping/)
- Established names in the early-stopping and learning-curve literature: "generalization loss", "training progress", "progress quotient" ([Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)); "learning curve extrapolation", "predictive termination criterion" ([Domhan et al. 2015](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/487.pdf)); "learning curve" (sample-wise) ([Viering & Loog](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.10948)); "training curves" ([Swersky et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.3896)).

### Inferences
- If the Greek analogy is kept, the per-epoch AUC slope is analogous to **theta** (sensitivity to time), and "gamma" as defined (change of the slope per epoch) has no standard Greek. Charm is the time derivative of delta, not the second time derivative of value.
- Recommended renames for the dashboard and docs:
  - delta → "AUC slope (ΔAUC/epoch)" or "rate of improvement";
  - gamma → "slope change" or "curvature" (marked as noisy);
  - "flat" → "plateau (slope < threshold)".

  Reserve "delta" for a threshold only if it means Keras-style `min_delta`. "Gamma" also collides in ML with, for example, the multiplicative LR-decay factor of PyTorch `StepLR`/`ExponentialLR`, the RL discount factor and the focal-loss focusing parameter. These are well known, but I did not re-fetch documentation for them in this session.

### Gaps
- I did not run a dedicated search for any ML paper or blog that uses options Greeks for training curves. Absence is inferred from not finding it in any source above, not from a targeted negative search.

## 7. Practical recommendations: epoch budgets and early stopping for a small head with a fixed annealed schedule

### Takeaway
With minute-long runs on cached features, the established and cheapest approach is to treat **total epochs as a schedule hyperparameter**. Run the full OneCycle schedule at a few budgets, compare the **end-of-schedule** pooled OOF macro AUC (report-label set for resolution, gold as arbiter), and pick the smallest budget within noise. Use the last (or SWA-averaged) checkpoint with one fixed epoch count for all folds. Do not use a "flat" epoch, per-fold best-epoch restoration, or a second-difference signal to set the budget.

### Cited Findings
- With a T-dependent schedule, the run length must "be known in advance and match the cosine length", and intermediate cosine points underestimate achievable performance. — [Hägele et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18392v3)
- A short cooldown (~20%) from a constant LR matches cosine, enabling final-score-vs-budget curves from one long run plus forked cooldowns; (1−sqrt) beats linear. — [Hägele et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18392v3)
- T-free schedules historically underperform T-dependent ones; Schedule-Free AdamW closes this gap via iterate averaging. — [Defazio et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15682)
- Longer or slower stopping buys small gains: about 4% lower error for about 4x training time, and even good criteria pick the best solution only 60–80% of the time. — [Prechelt](https://page.mi.fu-berlin.de/prechelt/Biblio/stop_tricks1997.pdf)
- Selecting on a finite validation sample causes selection bias comparable to real differences between methods. — [Cawley & Talbot 2010](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html)
- SWA and EMA give strong trajectory estimates at almost no cost, and EMA needs less LR decay. — [Izmailov et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.05407); [Morales-Brotons et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18704)

### Inferences
Concrete procedure (serves **S3**: iteration speed; and **S4**: one efficient model):
1. **Budget sweep, not a flat detector.** On fixed folds and seed, run full OneCycle at, for example, 8 / 12 / 16 / 24 epochs. Report end-of-schedule macro AUC on the 4,349 report-only OOF studies and on the 58 gold. Pick the smallest budget whose report-label AUC is within the measured seed noise of the best. Repeat only when the features or head change materially.
2. **Plot against fraction of schedule (or cumulative LR), not raw epoch,** when overlaying runs with different budgets. If delta is kept, compute it only on the pre-anneal or high-LR part and mask the last ~15–20% of the schedule, where it is ~0 by construction.
3. **Calibrate the "flat" threshold to noise.** Estimate the SD of per-epoch fold-averaged delta from 3–5 seed repeats. A threshold below about 2 SD is meaningless. A relative threshold such as delta/(1 − AUC) is more comparable across labels and runs. Drop gamma as a decision signal, or compute it from a fitted smooth curve.
4. **Checkpoint choice.** Use the last epoch, or SWA/EMA over the final ~20%, with the same fixed epoch count for all folds. Avoid per-fold best-epoch restoration on the scored validation fold (Cawley & Talbot). Log "best − last" on the report-label OOF as a sanity check.
5. **If anytime diagnostics are really needed,** switch to warmup → constant → (1−sqrt) cooldown of ~20% (WSD) or Schedule-Free AdamW. Delta is then interpretable during the constant phase, and several budgets can be evaluated from one run via forked cooldowns.
6. **Watch for epoch-wise double descent** from noisy report labels (S1). Run one long constant-LR run and check the report-label AUC for a dip-and-recover pattern before trusting any early "plateau" or "overfitting" flag.
7. **Efficiency track caveat (project-level inference):** if training happens offline and the Kaggle notebook only runs inference, the epoch budget affects iteration speed (S3), not the efficiency-track runtime (S4). Check `docs/competition.md` before optimising epochs for S4.

### Gaps
- These recommendations rest on general and LLM-scale evidence. I found no source testing them specifically for small attention heads on frozen medical-imaging features with ~12–28 epoch OneCycle runs. The budget sweep in step 1 is the project-specific test.
